Director school

Every camera choice starts with a story job.

Before you choose a wide shot, close-up, low angle, or tracking move, ask what the shot is doing for the audience. These three questions make camera language feel less technical and more human.

01

What should we feel?

Wonder, danger, comedy, loneliness, triumph, curiosity. The emotion chooses the lens before the lens chooses the shot.

02

Where should we look?

The frame is a magic trick. Size, angle, position, and light tell the audience what matters right now.

03

What changes by the end?

A shot should arrive somewhere: a discovery, a laugh, a decision, a reveal, or a final image that holds.

Director notes for AI video

Clear shots make stronger AI video scenes.

AI video tools respond best to visual decisions that are specific and easy to follow. These habits turn directing choices into plain scene language: where things are, what stays consistent, what moves, and how the shot ends.

01

Map the frame

Say where the subject sits: left, center, right, foreground, midground, or background. This helps stop the scene from feeling random.

Example: subject in the left third, empty beach in the right two-thirds.

02

Lock the subject

Keep identity, wardrobe, pose, gaze, and contact points stable. AI video needs clear anchors so characters do not drift or change.

Example: same face, same hoodie, hand stays on the table.

03

Plan the movement

Give the camera and subject one main motion. A push-in, track, orbit, pan, or static hold should support the emotion of the shot.

Example: slow push-in as the character realizes what they found.

04

Know the last frame

A good shot has an ending picture. Describe what the final frame should hold so the moment lands instead of wandering away.

Example: final close-up holds on the glowing shell in her palm.

05

Use real scene sound

Start with diegetic audio: sounds that belong inside the scene, like footsteps, room tone, waves, cloth movement, or dialogue.

Example: ocean ambience, soft sand steps, shell hum, no music.

06

Build short sequences

For multi-shot animation, think in timed beats. Three to seven shots across 15 seconds is usually easier to understand and refine.

Example: wide opener, medium action, close-up reaction, final detail.

Beginner sequence pattern

A simple 15-second learning structure

Use this as a starter rhythm for visual storytelling. Each shot changes size, angle, or movement because the story beat changes.

  1. 0-4s: wide shot establishes the location and subject.
  2. 4-8s: medium shot shows the main action clearly.
  3. 8-12s: close-up shows the emotional reaction.
  4. 12-15s: detail shot or held final frame lands the idea.

Practice on set

Build a shot like a director.

A strong scene starts with a simple set of choices. Use this checklist to turn an idea into a clear camera plan before writing a script, storyboard, or AI video prompt.

Set-ready checklist

  1. Name the feeling before naming the shot.
  2. Decide where the audience should look first.
  3. Give the camera one clean job.
  4. Hold a memorable final image.
  5. Keep action simple enough for the moment to read.
